What is actually in it

The size of it

Take it with you

A plate is an image with fifteen digits printed on its face, and those digits are the last fifteen digits of the number that image is. Not a caption — the picture and the number are the same object, so the picture can be solved for.

Fifteen digits. A birthday, a phone number, anything — the archive will find an image whose address ends with it.

The archive holds every image that can be drawn on its grid: every photograph ever taken and every one that never was, at every moment, from every angle — alongside an overwhelming preponderance of noise. Nothing is stored. An address is its image, so possession of the number is possession of the picture.

How an address works

Read the pixels row by row, each channel most-significant byte first, and treat the whole run as one integer in base 256. Address zero is black; the largest address is white; every image in between sits at exactly one number. The map is a bijection, which is what makes the collection complete rather than merely large.

Why there are two lanes

At 4K and 48-bit a full address runs to — decimal digits. That cannot be put in a link, so a coordinate — 128 bits, 32 hexadecimal characters — names an address through a counter-based function. Every coordinate resolves to a real address. Not every address has a coordinate: those are reached by supplying the image, which is what search does.

Why it is fast

The colour at a pixel depends only on that pixel's index, never on the ones before it. The GPU can therefore evaluate all 8,294,400 of them at once, and the CPU can read any byte of an address without walking the preceding forty-seven megabytes.

Is every image random?

No — and the difference is the whole point. The archive contains every photograph ever taken, pure black at address zero, pure white at the largest address, and every diagram, painting and page of text that fits the grid. Nothing in it is random; it is simply all of them.

What is random is the coordinate lane. Pressing Random draws from 2¹²⁸ coordinates, and every one of them resolves to noise — not because the generator prefers noise, but because images that mean anything are vanishingly rare among all images. You will never find a face this way. The Address panel measures the entropy of whatever is loaded, so you can check that claim on the picture in front of you rather than take it on faith.

There are no bad coordinates. The generator is counter-based, so it has no internal state to fall into a rut: every coordinate is an independent key, including all zeros. Tested across 5,000 random coordinates, 5 exceeded the p<0.001 threshold for non-uniformity — which is exactly the 5 that randomness predicts. Adjacent coordinates differ in 64.3 of 128 bits on average, against an ideal of 64.

Plane and sphere

A plane grid is a window: the address is everything visible through a frame pointed one way. A sphere grid is a standpoint — the same bytes, read as a full turn of longitude against half a turn of latitude, so the address is not a picture you look at but a place you look from. Drag to turn your head. Nothing about the bijection changes; only the reading of the grid does, which is why geometry belongs to the format and travels with the file.

It is a trade, not an upgrade, and the numbers are in the panel. Equirectangular sampling crowds texels together near the poles, so only about 64% of a sphere's address does distinct work — the rest is the projection's redundancy rather than the archive's content. And a default sphere resolves about 3.7× coarser per degree of view than a flat 4K frame at the same field of view. Completeness is bought with resolution.

How large the grid can get

Browsing costs one evaluation per pixel of your screen, not of the image, so a 64K grid draws as quickly as a 1080p one — two billion pixels in about five milliseconds. Resolving an address is the expensive direction: the bytes have to exist, and the picture of them has to fit in a texture. That caps out near 16K, above which a grid stays browsable but can never be held.

On colour depth

Channels are 16 bits, the deepest integer colour any mainstream still format carries, and the surface is Display-P3 with extended range where the browser allows it. Most monitors will show 8 or 10 of those bits. The other bits are not wasted: they are the difference between 16.8 million possible colours and 281.5 trillion, and the archive is that much larger for them.
